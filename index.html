01 · Foundations

One disease.
Two ways to lose vision.

Use this four-step mental model before you memorize any stage. If you can trace the pathway, the lesions and treatments start to make sense.

The mental model: metabolic injury → capillary damage → VEGF → leak or grow.

01

Metabolic stress

Chronic hyperglycemia activates AGEs, the polyol pathway, PKC, and oxidative stress. Capillaries take the hit.

Glucose → injury Recall
02

Capillary damage

Pericyte loss and basement-membrane thickening cause occlusion (ischemia) and leakage (edema).

Ischemia + leak Recall
03

VEGF rises

Hypoxia upregulates vascular endothelial growth factor. VEGF increases permeability and drives new vessel growth.

Hypoxia → VEGF Recall
04

Leak or grow

Leakage causes diabetic macular edema. Neovascularization causes proliferative disease and its complications.

    04 · Management

    Leak or grow?
    Treat the driver.

    DME is a macular-leakage problem. PDR is a neovascular-growth problem. They can coexist, so always check the macula and the periphery.

    Leak

    DME

    Diabetic macular edema. Fluid and thickening in the macula cause blurred vision, reduced contrast, and metamorphopsia.

    • Key test: OCT to detect and quantify center involvement.
    • Center-involving: anti-VEGF is first-line.
    • Non-center: observe or focal/grid laser; individualize.
    • Refractory: consider intravitreal steroid; treat systemic risk factors.
    Hook: leaky macula → anti-VEGF.
    Grow

    PDR

    Proliferative diabetic retinopathy. VEGF-driven new vessels are fragile and can bleed, fibrose, or cause traction.

    • Defining feature: neovascularization (NVD or NVE).
    • High-risk PDR: PRP; anti-VEGF is an alternative.
    • Vitreous hemorrhage: observe initially; vitrectomy if non-clearing.
    • Tractional detachment: urgent retina referral for vitrectomy.
    Hook: new vessels → PRP; blood or traction → vitrectomy.

    Case 1 · Blurred, distorted vision

    A 58-year-old with type 2 diabetes has 3 weeks of blurry, distorted vision. OCT shows central retinal thickening. What is the diagnosis and first-line treatment?

    Center-involving DME → anti-VEGF. Also optimize glucose, blood pressure, and lipids.

    Case 2 · Sudden floaters and vision loss

    A 34-year-old with type 1 diabetes reports sudden painless floaters and vision loss. The fundus view is obscured by blood. What complication is most likely?

    Vitreous hemorrhage from PDR. Urgent retina referral; consider vitrectomy if the blood does not clear.

    Case 3 · Four quadrants plus beading

    A 62-year-old has hemorrhages in all four quadrants and venous beading in two quadrants. There is no neovascularization. What stage is this?

    Very severe NPDR — two 4-2-1 criteria, but still nonproliferative. Close retina follow-up and systemic optimization.

    ↻

    Recall check: why anti-VEGF for DME but PRP for high-risk PDR? Anti-VEGF reduces permeability and growth; PRP ablates ischemic retina to lower VEGF drive.

    05 · Screening

    Start the clock
    at the right time.

    Duration is a major risk factor. The screening clock starts differently in type 1, type 2, and pregnancy — and follow-up tightens as disease worsens.

    +5

    Type 1

    Start screening 5 years after diagnosis, then annually if no retinopathy.

    0

    Type 2

    Screen at diagnosis, then annually if no retinopathy. The disease may have been present for years.

    ♀

    Pregnancy

    With pregestational diabetes, screen before conception or in the first trimester; monitor during pregnancy and recheck postpartum.

    Typical follow-up rhythm

    No DR12 months
    Mild NPDR9–12 months
    Moderate NPDR6 months
    Severe / very severe3–4 months or sooner
    PDR / DMERetina specialist

    Typical intervals; individualize for pregnancy, rapid progression, poor follow-up, or systemic disease. Pediatric and adolescent screening timing may differ.

    Final recall

    Teach it back in 30 seconds.

    Without looking: what is diabetic retinopathy, why does vision drop, and what are the two treatment targets?

    Reveal a model answer

    Diabetic retinopathy is microvascular damage from chronic hyperglycemia. Capillary occlusion causes retinal ischemia and raises VEGF; capillary leakage causes macular edema. Vision drops from DME, vitreous hemorrhage, tractional detachment, or neovascular glaucoma.

    Treatment: optimize glucose, blood pressure, and lipids. Anti-VEGF for center-involving DME. PRP or anti-VEGF for PDR. Vitrectomy for non-clearing vitreous hemorrhage or tractional detachment.
